Why I design

Products people depend on

I grew up where the simplest conveniences were often out of reach. That’s where my interest in design started: not in how things look, but in whether they work for the person who needs them.

I studied engineering and found my way to product design because I kept asking why things were built the way they were, and how they could work better. I’m drawn to products where getting it right isn’t about aesthetics. It decides whether someone can see a doctor, manage their savings, or do their job without fighting the tool.

What I believe

Complexity is not the enemy. Confusion is.

Most of the software I work on is complicated because the job behind it is complicated. Payroll has to handle every exception. A portfolio review has to hold every number. You can hide that behind a friendly screen, but it doesn’t disappear. It moves to the person using the product, and now they’re guessing.

I want these products to look simple and be readable at the same time. That means the whole truth on screen, in the order someone actually works, with the next step obvious. Simplicity that costs people the ability to do their job isn’t simplicity. They pay for it later, in support tickets and the spreadsheet they keep on the side.

So I fix the sequence before I cut the depth. I read the complaints before I run a workshop. And in health and money, I earn a bit of trust before I ask for anything, because people decide fast whether a product is safe.

Where it showed up

Four beliefs, and the projects that tested them

Each of these changed a decision I made, and each one paid off.

01

Complexity can’t always be removed. It has to be organised.

Expert users rarely want less. Analysts at Lenox Park needed every number; what they lacked was an order that matched how they compare. We kept the depth and fixed the sequence.

3×Faster time to insight, with nothing removed→

02

The support queue is a better brief than a brainstorm.

Complaints are research people have already done for you. At GoCo, the Reports rebuild started from the tickets, not the roadmap, and the tickets told us exactly where trust broke.

↓30%Support tickets after the Reports rebuild→

03

A design system is an operating model, not a Figma library.

Components were never GoCo’s problem; twelve teams making their own calls was. The work that mattered was the habit: new work starts from the library, and exceptions earn their way in.

12+Product areas speaking one language→

04

Earn trust before you ask for anything.

In health and money, people decide in seconds whether a product is safe. At DRO Health we stopped asking for medical details until the first booking, when the reason was obvious.
